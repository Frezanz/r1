# Truth-First Investigation

**ID:** R-TRUTH-001  
**Status:** Open investigation  
**Priority:** First foundational research target  
**LLM independence:** Required; no LLM is assumed to define, verify, or supply truth.

## Purpose

Investigate truth before using a truth-related mechanism to filter, connect, rank, or revise the framework's contents. The purpose is not to select a philosophical slogan and force all observations into it. The purpose is to compare candidate accounts, state their commitments, and find where each succeeds or fails.

## Keep these questions separate

1. **What is truth?** What kind of thing is truth, and what makes a claim true or false?
2. **What is a truth-bearer?** Is truth assessed for propositions, statements, models, observations, relations, or some other formal object? A sentence and the proposition it expresses must not be silently conflated.
3. **What makes it true?** Possible accounts may refer to reality, correspondence, coherence, proof, successful prediction, or other relations. Treat these as candidate accounts to compare, not synonyms.
4. **How can we know?** What evidence or procedure justifies accepting, rejecting, or suspending judgment about a claim?
5. **What can be measured?** Is truth itself measurable, or are evidence strength, error rate, calibration, consistency, predictive success, or robustness the measurable quantities?
6. **Under what conditions?** Which claims are unconditional, and which are conditional on definitions, scope, a reference frame, a model, or a time interval?
7. **How does it change?** Does the world change, does the claim's applicability change, does new evidence change our assessment, or does the meaning of the claim change? Record these as distinct possibilities.

## Candidate distinctions to preserve

- **Truth vs. truth assessment:** a claim's truth is not identical to our current judgment about it.
- **Truth vs. evidence:** evidence supports or undermines an assessment; evidence is not automatically the truth-maker.
- **Truth vs. confidence:** confidence describes an assessor's state or degree of belief, not necessarily reality.
- **Truth vs. consistency:** a set of claims may be internally consistent while being wrong about the world.
- **Truth vs. stability:** a false claim can remain stable; a true claim may be conditional or concern a changing situation.
- **Truth vs. usefulness/value:** utility is goal- and context-dependent and cannot by itself establish truth.
- **Absolute vs. conditional:** do not label a claim absolute merely because its conditions have not yet been stated. Search for hidden scope, definitions, and assumptions.
- **Unknown vs. false:** absence of adequate evidence does not automatically establish falsity.
- **Contradiction vs. apparent contradiction:** check whether claims differ in time, scope, definitions, reference, or perspective before treating them as a formal contradiction.

These are working distinctions for investigation, not a complete theory.

## Initial assessment record

For every claim admitted to the framework, record what is applicable:

- **Claim ID and exact content**
- **Content type:** observation, report, proposition, definition, inference, hypothesis, prediction, question, or value judgment
- **Interpretation:** what the terms mean
- **Scope and conditions:** domain, assumptions, reference/perspective, and applicable time or interval
- **Truth status:** asserted, supported, contradicted, conditional, or unresolved
- **Evidence:** observations, measurements, derivations, sources, and counterevidence
- **Assessment method:** how the claim was checked and what the method cannot establish
- **Confidence/uncertainty:** only when there is a defensible basis; keep separate from truth status
- **Provenance:** who/what supplied the item, when, and through which process
- **Dependencies:** premises, definitions, instruments, models, and other claims it relies on
- **Revision condition:** what could strengthen, weaken, qualify, or overturn it
- **Practical metadata:** time sensitivity, meaning, value, mattering, consequences, and intended use

A field may be unknown or inapplicable. Do not fabricate a value to complete the record.

## Truth assessment is not one universal score

Do not start with a single 0–100 truth score. Different methods answer different questions:

- Direct observation can establish that an observation was recorded, but interpretation and instrument limits remain.
- Measurement depends on operational definitions, calibration, uncertainty, and conditions.
- Deduction establishes a conclusion relative to premises and inference rules; it does not independently guarantee the premises describe reality.
- Repeatability and robustness test sensitivity to procedure or conditions.
- Prediction tests whether a model anticipates outcomes under stated conditions.
- Testimony and documents require source and provenance assessment.
- Formal consistency tests compatibility within a specified system, not necessarily correspondence to the world.

The framework should represent which assessment method was used and its limits. A numerical score may be useful for a specific calibrated task, but it must have a defined interpretation and validation.

## Framework-filter sequence

1. Preserve the item as received, including its source and timestamp when available.
2. Determine what kind of item it is; do not treat every phrase as a factual claim.
3. Parse its intended meaning and conditions; expose ambiguity rather than silently resolving it.
4. Identify evidence, derivation, source quality, and possible counterevidence.
5. Assign a justified epistemic status, allowing unresolved and conditional states.
6. Attach temporal applicability and revision history.
7. Map dependencies, relations, conflicts, and consequences.
8. Separately annotate meaning, value, mattering, urgency, and use.
9. Decide whether to store, link, defer, flag, retrieve, or prioritize it for the current task.
10. Record why the decision was made and what could change it.

Filtering should not mean deleting everything not currently verified. Unresolved claims may be valuable questions or hypotheses. They must remain distinguishable from established findings.

## Open questions to investigate first

1. Is truth a property of a proposition, a relation to reality, or another kind of structure?
2. What is the smallest well-defined object whose truth status the framework evaluates?
3. Can a claim be true without any available observer or assessment procedure?
4. What exactly does “measure truth” mean in different domains?
5. Which aspects can be measured directly, and which are inferred through a model or procedure?
6. How should the framework represent a claim that is true only during an interval or under a specified perspective?
7. When two sources conflict, what procedure distinguishes a real contradiction from different scopes or times?
8. What would count as evidence against our current account of truth?
9. How should truth assessment interact with value and relevance without collapsing into them?
10. Which parts of the framework filter are domain-general, and which require domain-specific rules?

## First test set

Use deliberately simple cases before complex philosophical examples:

- A claim with direct, repeatable observations.
- A claim supported by a source but not independently verified.
- A claim that is internally consistent but contradicted by observation.
- A claim that was true at one time but is no longer true.
- A conditional claim whose condition is omitted.
- Two apparently contradictory claims with different reference times.
- A claim with insufficient evidence.
- A useful prediction that is wrong.
- A true but practically irrelevant claim.
- A meaningful or valuable statement that is not a factual proposition.

For each case, ask whether the framework preserves the right distinctions and what information is missing.

## Completion criteria

This investigation is not complete until it has:
- compared multiple accounts of truth rather than presupposing one;
- defined the truth-bearer and assessment target;
- separated truth status from evidence, confidence, stability, value, and relevance;
- specified conditional and temporal scope;
- proposed testable procedures and counterexamples;
- stated what the framework cannot determine;
- recorded unresolved disputes instead of hiding them.

**No final theory of truth is asserted here.**
